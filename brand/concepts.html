Six other routes to the same mark

Each of these is a different idea, not a restyling of the one you have. They are drawn in the app's new cornflower blue so you can judge them against the interface they will sit in, and every one is put through the same three tests underneath: does it survive 24 pixels, does it survive being flattened to one colour, and does it survive being reversed out of a blue tile.

Where we are

The incumbent

Worth keeping on screen while you look at the rest — the question is not whether the alternatives are good, it is whether any of them is better than this.

CURRENT

Orbit Node

An infinity loop that is a real orbit: it passes behind the planet on the left and across its face on the right, so the figure-eight is a route rather than a symbol laid on top of a globe.

Strongest at: saying the slogan without words. Weakest at: small sizes — the weave is the whole idea and it is the first thing to close up.

The alternatives

Six directions

Ordered roughly from the most literal to the most abstract. Read the Flat proof carefully: a mark that survives being crushed to a single colour needs no special version for stamps, embroidery or a one-ink invoice. A mark that collapses into a blob there needs a second drawing made by hand.

01

Pin Sphere

The map pin everyone already reads as "a place", with a globe knocked out of its head instead of the usual dot. The most immediately legible thing on this page — nobody has to be told what it is.

Best for: app stores and maps, where it competes with a hundred other icons. Cost: it is also the least distinctive — pins are the most crowded shape in travel.

02

Latitude Stack

Seven bars whose widths are the chords of a circle, so the silhouette is a sphere built entirely out of lines of latitude. It is also seven days with one of them lit — the globe and the itinerary are the same drawing.

Best for: a product that is really a planner. Scales further down than anything else here. Cost: it needs a second of thought before the sphere appears.

03

Route Arc

A dotted flight path leaving a globe, with the departure marked in amber and the arrival as a wing. The most conventional idea on the page and the one whose meaning is nearest the surface.

Best for: being understood in half a second by someone who has never seen it. Cost: it is the shape three of your competitors already use.

04

Meridian P

A monogram that only works because of the name: the bowl of the P is already a circle, so it costs nothing to make it the planet. One latitude line and the node are the entire intervention.

Best for: square crops, merchandise, a favicon that has to hold its own beside a wordmark. Cost: a monogram says the name, not the promise.

Stub Sphere

A planet with a boarding pass's perforation running down it. Two objects that have nothing to do with each other, sharing one outline — the globe is the ticket. The idea with the most personality here.

Best for: a brand that wants to be remembered rather than understood. Cost: the perforation is fine detail, and fine detail is what dies first.

06

Compass Node

A four-point star inside a ring: the oldest travel symbol there is, drawn tight enough to read as a solid object rather than an illustration. The ring is the sphere, the star is the bearing.

Best for: the widest range of sizes and surfaces of anything here. Cost: it is a compass. It says direction, not infinity, and not planning.

If you want my answer

Keep the orbit, add the stack

Latitude Stack, as the small-size half of a pair

Nothing on this page beats Orbit Node at what the brand is actually about — it is the only mark that draws the slogan rather than illustrating the category. I would not replace it.

But its weakness is real and it is the weakness that costs you most: the weave is the idea, and the weave is the first thing to close at favicon size. Latitude Stack solves exactly that and nothing else. It survives 24px better than anything here, it needs no separate one-colour drawing, and it carries the same two facts — a sphere, and days you can plan — in a form that is all silhouette.

So: Orbit Node stays the mark. Latitude Stack becomes the icon it degrades into, the way a wordmark degrades into a monogram. One idea, two draw weights, and the favicon stops being an apology.

If you would rather have one mark and no pair, take Meridian P — it is the only single mark here that passes every mechanical test without giving up the name.
